How to build your 2027 budget from your 2026 numbers

You don't need a finance team or a blank spreadsheet to plan next year. The fastest honest budget starts from what actually happened this year, month by month, then changes only the few lines you know will be different. Here's how to do it in about an hour.

Checked November 18, 2026

Why start from last year

A budget built from scratch is mostly guesses. Your own books already show your seasons: the slow January, the busy spring, the month insurance renews. Starting each month of 2027 from the same month of 2026 keeps those patterns, so the plan looks like your business instead of twelve equal slices.

You only need three lines to start: revenue, cost of goods sold (materials, subcontractors, freight, direct labor) and operating expenses (rent, salaries, insurance, software, marketing). You can add detail later.

Step 1: Pull 2026 by month

Run a profit and loss report by month for January to December 2026 from QuickBooks® or Xero. In November and December, the last months of the year aren't booked yet. Use the same months from 2025 for those, so the year is complete.

Step 2: Pick two growth rates

Use one rate for sales and one for overhead. Keep them separate, because they move for different reasons.

  • Sales growth should come from what you know: signed contracts, price increases you've announced, a new crew or location. If you can't name the reason, use a lower number.
  • Overhead growth should reflect rising costs. For reference, U.S. consumer prices were up 3.7% in the 12 months to September 2026, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics. Your own costs may rise faster or slower: check your insurance renewal, lease and payroll first.

Let cost of goods grow with sales, so your gross margin stays where it was unless you have a reason to change it.

Step 3: Apply them month by month

Example only, not a benchmark. 2026 actuals: Revenue $2,400,000 COGS $1,560,000 Overhead $660,000 Operating profit $180,000 (7.5% of revenue) 2027 plan: Sales +6%, overhead +4% Revenue $2,400,000 × 1.06 = $2,544,000 COGS $1,560,000 × 1.06 = $1,653,600 Overhead $660,000 × 1.04 = $686,400 Operating profit = $204,000 (8.0% of revenue) By month: March 2026 revenue $180,000 → March 2027 $190,800 November not booked yet: November 2025 $210,000 × 1.06 × 1.06 = $235,956

Notice that overhead growing slower than sales lifts the margin from 7.5% to 8.0%. If overhead grows faster than sales, the margin shrinks even when revenue rises. That's the first thing a plan should show you.

Step 4: Adjust the lines you know will change

Now go line by line and change only what's different from a normal year:

  • New hires in the month they start, at salary plus payroll taxes and benefits.
  • Lease and insurance renewals at the new price, in the month they take effect.
  • Big one-time costs such as equipment, a vehicle or a software switch.
  • A lost or won customer. Take a lost customer's sales out, month by month.
  • Loan payments. These don't show on the P&L as an expense, but they use cash. Plan for them in your cash forecast.

Step 5: Turn the budget into a cash plan

A budget shows profit, not cash. A month can be profitable on paper while the bank account drops, because customers pay 30 to 60 days after you do the work. For the next three months, put your budget into a 13-week cash forecast. It shows when money actually comes in and goes out. Then check how long your cash lasts in a slow month with the cash runway calculator.

Step 6: Check it every month

The budget is only useful if you compare it with what happens. Each month, look at revenue, cost of goods and overhead against plan. Only dig into lines that missed by a meaningful amount, for example more than 5% and more than $1,000. Then write one sentence on why. After three months, you'll know whether to raise or lower the rest of the year.

Common mistakes

  1. Splitting the year into twelve equal months. It hides your seasons, and every slow month looks like a crisis.
  2. Hoping instead of planning. A 20% sales jump with no named reason isn't a plan. Plan for the likely case, then add an upside case separately.
  3. Forgetting cost increases. Insurance, rent and wages rarely stay flat.
  4. Never looking at it again. A budget you don't compare with actuals is just a document.
