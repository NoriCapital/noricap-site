Where to get funding for a small business: every option compared

Most owners call one bank, hear no, and stop. There are at least eight places to look, and each one says yes to a different kind of business. Here's who lends, roughly how much, what it costs, and which one to try first.

Checked November 16, 2026

The short answer

  • Profitable, two or more years in business, good records? Start with a bank or credit union. It's usually the cheapest money.
  • Good business, but the bank wants more collateral or a longer track record? Ask the same bank about an SBA-backed loan.
  • Small amount, newer business, or turned down by banks? Look at a CDFI or an SBA microloan lender.
  • Buying a truck or machine? Equipment finance, where the equipment backs the loan.
  • Waiting on customers who pay slowly? A line of credit first; factoring if you can't get one.
  • Need it fast, whatever the cost? Online lenders, but read the total cost before you sign.

Who applies, and who gets approved

The Federal Reserve's 2026 survey of employer firms found that sixty percent of firms applied for financing in the prior 12 months, and only 42 percent of applicants received the full amount they sought. Where you apply matters: applicants at small banks were more likely to be fully approved (57%), and credit union and bank applicants were more satisfied than online lender and finance company applicants. The survey is a convenience sample, so treat the figures as a guide, not a forecast for your business.

1. Banks

Term loans, lines of credit and commercial real estate loans. Banks want two or three years of tax returns, steady profits and enough earnings to cover the payments with room to spare. Many look for a debt service coverage ratio of about 1.25x; our DSCR guide shows how to work yours out. Small and community banks often know local businesses better than large ones, so if a large bank says no, try a local one before giving up on banks.

2. SBA-backed loans

The SBA doesn't usually lend directly. It guarantees part of a loan made by a bank or other lender, which lets the lender say yes to deals it would otherwise turn down, often with longer terms. The main options, from SBA's lender page:

  • 7(a): the general-purpose loan. The maximum loan amount for a 7(a) loan is $5 million. It can cover working capital, equipment, real estate or buying a business.
  • 7(a) Small: term loans of $350,000 or less.
  • SBA Express: up to $500,000, with a smaller SBA guarantee and a simpler process for the lender.
  • 504: fixed-rate financing for buildings, land and long-life equipment, made with a Certified Development Company. SBA's lender table lists the 504 at $25,000 to $5.5 million. It can't be used for working capital or inventory. See SBA 7(a) vs 504.
  • Microloans: covered under CDFIs below.

SBA's site has a free tool to get matched to an SBA-approved lender (Lender Match).

3. CDFIs and SBA microloans

Community Development Financial Institutions are mission-driven financial institutions certified by the U.S. Treasury. The CDFI Coalition counted 1,383 Treasury-certified CDFIs in January 2026: loan funds, credit unions, banks and venture funds. They lend to businesses that banks pass on, such as newer firms, owners with thin credit, and businesses in underserved areas, and many include coaching.

Many also make SBA microloans. Per SBA, the program provides loans up to $50,000, the average microloan is about $13,000, and rates are generally between 8%-13%, set by the nonprofit lender.

4. Credit unions

If you already bank with a credit union, ask what it offers businesses. Federal rules cap a credit union's total business lending at about 1.75 times its net worth, so some lend a lot to businesses and others very little. In the Fed survey, credit union applicants were among the most satisfied.

5. State and local programs

Most states run loan programs, many funded through Treasury's State Small Business Credit Initiative (SSBCI). The 2021 version provides a combined $10 billion to states, territories and Tribal governments for loan participation, loan guarantee, collateral support, capital access and venture capital programs. Usually you don't apply to the state: you apply to a bank, and the state program helps that bank say yes, for example by backing part of the collateral. Ask your lender whether it takes part in your state's program. Cities, counties and economic development groups often run small revolving loan funds too. Programs change and funds run out, so check the official page before you apply.

6. Online lenders

Fast and easy to apply for, often funding in days. The trade-off is cost: sixty percent of those that borrowed from online lenders said the actual cost was higher than they expected. Merchant cash advances, which take a share of daily card sales or bank deposits, are often quoted as a "factor rate" rather than an interest rate, which makes them hard to compare. Before you sign, ask for the total dollars you'll repay and how long you'll have, then compare that against a bank quote.

7. Equipment finance

Loans or leases where the truck, machine or vehicle backs the debt. Because the lender can take back the equipment, approval can be easier than for an unsecured loan, and the payment is often spread over the equipment's useful life. Dealers, banks and specialist finance companies all offer it. Compare the loan against a lease, including what happens at the end of the lease.

8. Factoring

You sell unpaid invoices to a factoring company for cash now. One provider describes typical terms as 80% to 90% of the invoice value paid within a day or two, minus a fee of 1% to 5% per 30 days, with industry data clustering around 2.5% a month. That sounds small. Here's what it means over a year.

Worked example: factoring vs a line of credit

The figures are made up for illustration, including the 10% line of credit rate. Swap in real quotes.

Invoice $100,000 Advance at 90% $90,000 Fee at 2.5% for 30 days $2,500 Paid back to you when the customer pays: $100,000 − $90,000 − $2,500 = $7,500 Fee ÷ cash you got: $2,500 ÷ $90,000 = 2.78% a month 2.78% × 12 months = about 33% a year Same $90,000 from a line of credit at 10% for 30 days: $90,000 × 10% × 30 ÷ 365 = $739.73 Extra cost of factoring: $2,500 − $739.73 = $1,760.27

Factoring can still make sense when a bank won't offer a line, or when the factor also handles collections. But if you factor every month, the cost adds up, and a line of credit is worth chasing.

What about grants?

Be careful here. In SBA's own words, SBA does not provide grants for starting and expanding a business. Real grants exist, such as federal research grants (SBIR and STTR) and some state and local programs, but they are narrow, competitive and slow. Anyone who asks for a fee to "unlock" a government grant is very likely running a scam.

Before you apply anywhere

  1. Know how much you can carry. The how much can I borrow calculator shows what your earnings can support.
  2. Know when you need it. The 13-week cash flow template shows the week cash gets tight, so you apply before you're desperate, when the cheaper options are still open.
  3. Get your documents ready. Our loan application checklist lists what lenders ask for.
  4. Get more than one quote. Compare total dollars repaid, not just the rate.
