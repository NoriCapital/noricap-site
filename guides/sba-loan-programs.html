SBA loan programs explained: 7(a), 7(a) Small, Express, 504, Microloan and CAPLines

The SBA runs several loan programs, and the names blur together. Each one fits a different job: a building, a line of credit, a small first loan, or a big mixed-use loan. Here's what each one does, the limits, what it costs, and which to ask your lender about.

Checked November 17, 2026

The short answer

  • Need up to $5 million for almost anything (working capital, equipment, real estate, buying a business)? Standard 7(a).
  • Need $350,000 or less? A 7(a) Small loan: same uses, bigger SBA guarantee on the smallest loans.
  • Need up to $500,000 and your bank offers it? SBA Express, including revolving lines of credit.
  • Buying or building property or long-life equipment? The 504, with a fixed rate on part of the loan and usually 10% down.
  • Need $50,000 or less, or turned down by banks? An SBA Microloan from a nonprofit lender.
  • Need working capital tied to seasons, contracts or building projects? CAPLines.

How SBA loans work

For almost all of these programs, the SBA doesn't hand you the money. A bank, credit union or other approved lender makes the loan, and the SBA guarantees part of it. If you don't repay, the SBA covers the lender for that share. That guarantee is what lets a lender say yes to a business it would otherwise turn down, or offer a longer term.

In SBA's words, "You will always work directly with your lender and not with SBA." So the first step for every program below is finding a lender that makes that kind of loan. SBA's free Lender Match tool connects you with SBA lenders. The exceptions are the 504, where a nonprofit Certified Development Company (CDC) works alongside a bank, and Microloans, which come from nonprofit intermediaries.

1. Standard 7(a): the all-purpose loan

The 7(a) is SBA's main program. The maximum loan amount for a 7(a) loan is $5 million. SBA's lender page treats loans of $350,001 to $5 million as "standard" 7(a), with a 75% guarantee.

What it can pay for, per SBA: real estate and buildings, short- and long-term working capital, refinancing business debt, machinery and equipment, furniture and supplies, and changes of ownership (complete or partial). You can mix these in one loan.

How long: 10 years or less, unless the loan finances real estate or equipment with a longer useful life. Real estate can run up to 25 years.

Rate: you and the lender negotiate it, but it can't exceed SBA's cap. For variable-rate loans the cap is set above a base rate, and the allowed margin shrinks as the loan grows:

Loan size Highest variable rate $50,000 or less base rate + 6.5% $50,001 to $250,000 base rate + 6.0% $250,001 to $350,000 base rate + 4.5% Over $350,000 base rate + 3.0%

Source: SBA's 7(a) page and lender page. Fixed-rate caps are published separately by SBA. Since March 1, 2026, SBA has also allowed additional base rate options, so ask your lender which base rate your loan uses.

SBA processing time: SBA lists 5-10 business days for standard 7(a) loans. Your lender's own underwriting comes on top.

Buying a business with a 7(a)? From October 1, 2026, buyers face tighter equity rules. Our 7(a) vs 504 guide covers the down payment.

2. 7(a) Small: loans up to $350,000

Same uses as a standard 7(a), for loans up to $350,000. The guarantee is 85% for loans up to $150,000 and 75% for loans greater than $150,000, and SBA lists a turnaround of 2-10 business days.

3. SBA Express: up to $500,000, smaller guarantee

SBA Express loans go up to $500,000, with an SBA guarantee of only 50%. So the lender carries more of the risk and may want a stronger borrower. Express can be a term loan or a revolving line of credit; lines can run up to 10 years. Not every SBA lender offers Express, so ask.

Exporters have their own versions, Export Express and the Export Working Capital Program, with larger guarantees; ask an SBA lender that does international trade.

4. 504: buildings, land and big equipment

A 504 is really two loans plus your money. A bank makes a first loan covering up to 50% of the project cost, a CDC makes a second loan covering up to 40% of the total cost, and you put in at least 10% equity.

  • Size: SBA's 504 page says the maximum loan amount for a 504 loan is $5.5 million (on the CDC part; elsewhere on the same page it says $5 million, and the higher figure applies to some projects such as manufacturers). The bank's part sits on top.
  • Uses: buying, building or renovating buildings and land, and machinery with a long useful life. It can't be used for working capital or inventory, or for rental real estate.
  • Terms: 10-, 20- and 25-year maturity terms are available.
  • Rate: fixed on the CDC part, pegged to 10-year U.S. Treasury rates. Fees total approximately 3% of the debt and can be financed in the loan.

For a full comparison with a worked example, see SBA 7(a) vs 504.

5. Microloans: up to $50,000

SBA Microloans come from nonprofit intermediary lenders, many of them community lenders, rather than banks. Per SBA:

  • Loans up to $50,000; the average microloan is about $13,000.
  • Rates are generally between 8%-13%, set by the lender.
  • Repay over no more than six years.
  • Can pay for working capital, inventory, supplies, furniture, fixtures, machinery and equipment. They cannot be used to pay existing debts or to purchase real estate.

They suit newer businesses, owners with thin credit, or anyone who needs a small amount a bank won't bother with.

6. CAPLines: working capital lines for specific needs

CAPLines are part of the 7(a) program, built for short-term and cyclical cash needs. SBA's lender page describes four types:

  • Seasonal: covers the seasonal build-up of receivables and inventory, and sometimes the extra labor that comes with it.
  • Contract: covers the costs of one or more specific contracts, including overhead tied to them.
  • Builders: for small general contractors building or renovating residential or commercial property for resale.
  • Working: an asset-based revolving line, backed by receivables and inventory, for businesses that can't meet the credit standards for long-term loans. Expect extra fees for the lender's monitoring of that collateral.

Except for Builders lines, CAPLines can run up to 10 years. Builders lines are tied to the length of the construction project. If you're a contractor or a seasonal business, CAPLines are worth asking about before you turn to factoring or a cash advance.

What SBA loans cost in fees

On top of interest, most 7(a) loans carry an upfront SBA guarantee fee, charged on the guaranteed part of the loan, not the whole loan. The lender usually passes it to you, and it can often be financed. For loans that get an SBA loan number from October 1, 2026 (fiscal year 2027), as summarized by the lenders' trade group NAGGL from SBA's fee notice:

Loan size (over 12 months) Upfront fee on guaranteed part $150,000 or less 2% $150,001 to $700,000 3% $700,001 to $5 million 3.5% up to $1M guaranteed, plus 3.75% above $1M 12 months or less 0.25%

NAGGL also reports a 0% upfront fee for SBA Express loans to veteran-owned businesses, and for loans of $700,000 or less to manufacturers, certain food supply chain businesses and businesses in rural areas. Ask your lender whether you qualify.

Worked example: what the upfront fee comes to

These loans are made up for illustration. Your lender will confirm the exact fee.

7(a) Small, $120,000 (85% guaranteed) Guaranteed part: $120,000 × 85% = $102,000 Fee: $102,000 × 2% = $2,040 SBA Express, $300,000 (50% guaranteed) Guaranteed part: $300,000 × 50% = $150,000 Fee: $150,000 × 3% = $4,500 Standard 7(a), $500,000 (75% guaranteed) Guaranteed part: $500,000 × 75% = $375,000 Fee: $375,000 × 3% = $11,250 Standard 7(a), $2,000,000 (75% guaranteed) Guaranteed part: $2,000,000 × 75% = $1,500,000 First $1,000,000 × 3.5% = $35,000 Next $500,000 × 3.75% = $18,750 Fee = $53,750

Notice the Express loan's fee is lower than a same-size 7(a) would be, because only half is guaranteed. On a big loan, the fee is real money: build it into your numbers before you sign.

Before you apply

  1. Check the payment fits. Lenders test whether your earnings cover the new payment with room to spare; see our DSCR guide, the how much can I borrow calculator and the loan payment calculator.
  2. Apply early. SBA loans take weeks, not days. The 13-week cash flow template shows when cash gets tight.
  3. Get your documents ready with our loan application checklist, and ask two or three lenders. For options beyond the SBA, see where to get funding for a small business.
