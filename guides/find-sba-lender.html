How to find an SBA lender (Lender Match and preferred lenders)

SBA doesn't lend you the money. A bank, credit union or other approved lender does, and SBA guarantees part of the loan. So the lender you pick decides how fast you hear back, which programs you can use and what rate you pay. Here's where to look and how to choose.

Checked November 26, 2026

The short answer

  • You apply with a lender, not SBA. SBA says: "You will always work directly with your lender and not with SBA."
  • Start with Lender Match. It's SBA's free tool. You answer questions in about five minutes, and SBA sends you a list of interested lenders two business days later.
  • Ask your own bank too. If it makes SBA loans, it already knows your business.
  • Look for a preferred lender. Lenders in SBA's Preferred Lenders Program (PLP) can approve your loan themselves, without waiting for SBA to review it.
  • Match the lender to the program. 504 loans come only through Certified Development Companies, and microloans through nonprofit intermediaries. SBA keeps a list of each.
  • Compare at least two offers on rate, fees, term and collateral.

Step 1: Use Lender Match

Lender Match is a free questionnaire on SBA's website. It works in four steps:

  1. Describe your needs. SBA says it takes "as little as five minutes".
  2. Get matched. SBA prepares "a summary of all the interested lenders two business days after you submit".
  3. Talk to lenders. Some will contact you, and you can contact them.
  4. Apply with the lender you choose, using its own application.

More than 800 lenders take part, across all 50 states and U.S. territories, according to SBA. Many of them also offer regular, non-SBA loans.

Lender Match isn't a loan application, and it doesn't promise a match. It's a way to find lenders who are interested in loans like yours. Use it to build a short list, not to wait for a single answer.

Step 2: Ask your own bank

Your current bank sees your deposits, payments and history. If it makes SBA loans, it may be the fastest place to start. Ask three plain questions:

  • Do you make SBA 7(a) loans, and which kinds (standard, Express, CAPLines)?
  • Are you a preferred (PLP) lender?
  • How many SBA loans did you make last year, and how long do they usually take?

A bank that makes only a few SBA loans a year may be slow or unsure of the rules. That's not a reason to rule it out, but it's a reason to get a second quote.

What a preferred (PLP) lender is

SBA gives some lenders "delegated authority". Under the Preferred Lenders Program, the lender is "responsible for all PLP loan decisions regarding eligibility (including size) and creditworthiness". It approves the loan, then notifies SBA, and SBA adds its guarantee unless it finds an eligibility problem.

A lender without that authority sends your loan to SBA for review before it can be approved. That adds a step. So if speed matters, ask each lender whether it is a PLP lender and whether your loan will be decided under its own authority.

SBA decides which lenders get this authority and for how long, based on their record. A lender can lose it, so ask again if months have passed since you last checked.

  • 504 loans "are available exclusively through Certified Development Companies (CDCs)", which SBA certifies and regulates. Find one on SBA's list of CDCs, filtered by state. See our 7(a) vs 504 guide to choose between them.
  • Microloans come from nonprofit intermediaries. Find them on SBA's list of microlenders. Our microloan guide covers rates and uses.

Step 4: Get free local help

Your SBA district office can answer questions about applying, and SBA's resource partners give free or low-cost help: Small Business Development Centers (SBDCs), SCORE mentors, Women's Business Centers and Veterans Business Outreach Centers. They often know which local lenders are active and can review your package before you send it.

Step 5: Compare offers the right way

SBA sets ceilings, not prices. The lender sets your rate and terms within SBA's limits. For variable-rate 7(a) loans, the highest rate a lender can charge is a base rate (usually prime) plus a margin that depends on loan size, under SBA's rulebook, SOP 50 10 8.1:

Loan size Highest variable rate $50,000 or less Prime + 6.5% $50,001 to $250,000 Prime + 6.0% $250,001 to $350,000 Prime + 4.5% Over $350,000 Prime + 3.0%

SBA also charges an upfront guarantee fee on the guaranteed part of the loan. For loans approved from October 1, 2026, it's 2% for loans over 12 months of $150,000 or less, and 3% from $150,001 to $700,000, as summarized by the lenders' trade group NAGGL. The lender usually passes it to you.

For each offer, write down the rate and how it's set (fixed, or prime plus what), the term, the guarantee fee, any other lender fees, the collateral and personal guarantees asked for, and any prepayment penalty. SBA itself suggests asking about "prepayment penalties, grace periods, and if/when the lender can demand full repayment".

Get ready before you call

Lenders move faster when the package is complete. SBA's own checklist asks you to know how much you need and what for, and to bring financial projections, your credit history and details of any collateral. Most lenders also want two or three years of tax returns, recent financial statements and a list of your current debts.
