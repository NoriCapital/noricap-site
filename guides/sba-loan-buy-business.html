SBA loans to buy a business: the 1.25x coverage rule from October 2026

An SBA 7(a) loan is the most common way to buy a small business with little money down. The rules got stricter on October 1, 2026. The business you're buying now has to show, on its past numbers, that it earns at least $1.25 for every $1 of loan payments. Here's what that means and how to check a deal before you sign.

Checked November 27, 2026

The short answer

  • Yes, SBA 7(a) loans can pay to buy a business. SBA lists "changes of ownership (complete or partial)" as an allowed use. The most a 7(a) loan can be is $5 million.
  • Coverage of 1.25x, on past earnings. For most purchases, the business's cash flow must cover all the debt payments after the sale at least 1.25 times. Before October 1 it was 1.15x. Projections can't make up a shortfall.
  • At least 10% down. SBA requires an equity contribution of at least 10% of total project costs. Seller notes and outside investors can cover no more than half of it, except for eligible veteran buyers through September 2027.
  • Paperwork goes up with price. Every purchase needs an independent business valuation. From a $3 million purchase price, the lender also needs a quality of earnings report.
  • Check the numbers before you sign a letter of intent. A price the business's earnings can't cover at 1.25x won't get an SBA loan.

What changed on October 1, 2026

SBA's rulebook for these loans is called SOP 50 10. Version 8.1 applies to loans that get an SBA loan number on or after October 1, 2026. For buying a business, law firm PilieroMazza summarizes the main changes this way:

  • Required coverage for "most change of ownership transactions" rises from 1.15x to 1.25x, and "projections generally will not be able to make up for a shortfall in historical cash flow".
  • Every purchase is underwritten as a standard 7(a) loan, whatever its size. The lighter treatment for loans of $350,000 or less no longer applies. That means a full credit review, an independent valuation and site visits.
  • Non-cash equity, such as seller notes, can't be more than half of the minimum equity injection.

Which coverage ratio applies to your deal

SBA now sorts purchases into types. A lender-focused summary by appraiser Daniel Smith gives the minimums:

Type of purchase Minimum coverage Buying a business outright 1.25x (initial acquisition) Buying out a partner or co-owner 1.25x Sale to an ESOP or co-op 1.25x Business expansion 1.15x

For a purchase, "the coverage floor is 1.25x, measured on either the last fiscal year end or an average of the last two". Projections "may be reviewed, but they may not be relied upon to clear the floor."

Coverage is the business's cash flow divided by all its loan payments after the sale. That includes the new SBA loan, any debt the business keeps, and any seller note that is being paid. These are SBA's minimums. A lender can ask for more.

The down payment: 10%, and who can pay it

SBA says buyers of an existing business must provide "an equity contribution equal to at least 10% of total project costs". Total project costs usually include the price plus closing costs and any working capital you borrow.

  • At least half from you. Under the general rule, seller standby notes, investor money and other outside equity "could provide no more than half of that contribution", SBA says. The rest has to be your own money, such as savings.
  • A seller note counts only if it's on full standby. The note must be "on full standby (no payments of principal or interest for the term of the 7(a) loan)", as quoted by Pioneer Capital Advisory. The seller also gives up its lien rights to the lender.
  • Other seller notes are allowed, but they don't count toward the 10%, and their payments count in the coverage test.
  • Veterans: from Veterans Day, November 11, 2026, through fiscal year 2027 (September 30, 2027), SBA lets eligible veteran buyers raise "the full required amount from permitted outside sources". Ask your lender whether you qualify.

Valuation and quality of earnings

Every purchase needs an independent valuation of the business. If the valuation comes in below the price, the gap has to be covered with more equity, according to Pioneer Capital Advisory's reading of the SOP.

When the purchase price is $3 million or more, the lender must also get a quality of earnings report. Accounting firm EisnerAmper says it must be prepared by an independent professional "engaged by the lender", not by the buyer or seller, and it includes a "cash proof" that ties bank deposits to tax returns. Owner buyouts and ESOP sales are exempt. The $3 million is measured on the price for the business, not counting appraised real estate the business occupies.

If the seller stays on

A seller can stay on as a consultant to help with the handover for up to 24 months in total, up from 12, according to the lender summary. Plan the handover with that limit in mind.

Worked example: a deal that passes the old rule but fails the new one

This business, its numbers and the rate are made up to show the arithmetic. Your lender will set the actual rate and term.

Purchase price plus costs $2,100,000 Your equity (10%) $210,000 at least half your own cash $105,000 up to half a full-standby seller note $105,000 SBA 7(a) loan $1,890,000 Assume 9.75%, 10 years, monthly payments Monthly payment $24,715.58 Yearly payments $296,586.91 (the standby note pays nothing) Business cash flow last year $360,000 Coverage: $360,000 / $296,586.91 1.21x Old rule, 1.15x passes New rule, 1.25x fails

What it would take to pass at 1.25x:

Most yearly payments allowed $360,000 / 1.25 $288,000 Largest loan at 9.75% over 10 years that payment supports $1,835,279.92 Short by $1,890,000 - $1,835,279.92 $54,720.08

To close, the buyer needs about $55,000 less debt: a lower price, or more cash. A regular seller note wouldn't help, because its payments count in the test too. Run your own numbers with the how much can I borrow calculator and the loan payment calculator.

How to check a deal before you make an offer

  1. Get the last two years of tax returns and financial statements for the business. The test uses past results.
  2. Work out cash flow the way a lender will. Start with EBITDA, add back only costs that truly end with the sale, and take off a fair salary for whoever will run it.
  3. Add up every payment after the sale: the SBA loan, debt the business keeps, and any paid seller note.
  4. Divide. If the result is under 1.25x on last year or the two-year average, lower the price or the debt before you sign.
  5. Count your cash. You need at least 5% of project costs from your own money (half of the 10%), plus enough to run the business after closing.
  6. Talk to an SBA lender early. Our guide to finding an SBA lender covers Lender Match and preferred lenders, and the application checklist lists the documents. The free 13-week cash flow template helps you plan the first months after you take over.
