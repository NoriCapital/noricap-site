Local small business loans: city and county loan funds, economic development groups and how to find them

Many cities, counties and regional development groups lend money to local businesses, often at lower rates than a bank and for owners a bank turned down or only half funded. They're easy to miss because each one is small and run by a different office. Here's who runs them, what they typically offer, who qualifies, and how to find the ones near you.

Checked November 19, 2026

The short answer

  • Who lends: city and county economic development offices, regional development districts, local development corporations and nonprofit lenders that run loan funds for them.
  • Where the money comes from: often federal grants (the Economic Development Administration's revolving loan funds, or HUD's Community Development Block Grant), plus city money such as tax increment financing.
  • Typical deal: smaller loans, often in the tens of thousands up to a few hundred thousand dollars, frequently used as "gap" money next to a bank loan.
  • Catch: you must be located in the area, the fund may want jobs created or kept, and money runs in cycles. When it's gone, the fund closes until loans are repaid.

Where local loan money comes from

EDA revolving loan funds

The U.S. Economic Development Administration gives grants "to establish revolving loan funds (RLFs) that make loans to businesses that cannot otherwise obtain traditional bank financing." EDA calls these loans "gap financing to enable small businesses to grow." The grant goes to a state, county, city, tribe, development district, college or nonprofit, which lends it out. As loans are repaid, the money is lent again, which is why it's called revolving.

Across the country, EDA reports $1.3 billion in total investment and 45,000 loans through these funds. To qualify, you generally must show that "credit is not otherwise available on terms and conditions that permit the completion or successful operation of the activity." In plain English: a bank won't fund all of it on workable terms.

Community Development Block Grant (CDBG) money

Cities and counties that receive HUD block grants may use them for "assistance to a private for-profit business, including, but not limited to, grants, loans, loan guarantees, interest supplements, technical assistance." These loans come with job rules. Across its whole program, a city must create or keep at least one full-time job per $35,000 of block grant money used, and a single deal generally can't cost more than $50,000 per job. That's why many CDBG-funded loans ask how many jobs you'll add or keep, and who fills them.

City money and tax increment financing

Some cities lend their own money, or money from tax increment financing (TIF) districts, where future property tax growth in a neighborhood pays for development there. These loans are usually tied to a specific district and often to real estate.

Certified Development Companies and CDFIs

Two other local lenders are worth knowing. Certified Development Companies are "SBA's community-based nonprofit partners who promote economic development within their communities"; they make SBA 504 loans for buildings and equipment, and many also run local loan funds. Community development financial institutions (CDFIs) are certified loan funds, credit unions and banks that lend in underserved areas; many cities hire them to run their programs.

What real local programs look like

These are examples of current programs, taken from their official pages on November 19, 2026. They show the range. Terms change and funds open and close, so check with the program before applying.

  • Ulster County, New York: the county revolving loan fund lends from $7,500 for micro loans and up to $75,000 for "gap" loans to businesses, with rates tied to the prime rate and terms of 6 to 15 years depending on what you buy. It wants at least three years of operating revenue and a business that's profitable after the owner's salary.
  • Madison, Wisconsin: the city's Small Cap TIF loan lends up to $250,000 at 0% for five years to buy or improve real estate. The balance is forgiven 20% a year while the business stays in the district. It's limited to for-profit businesses with 5 to 100 full-time-equivalent employees in or near certain TIF districts.
  • Norfolk, Virginia: the Norfolk revolving loan fund lends $5,000 to $50,000 (up to $100,000 in exceptional cases) to businesses with fewer than 25 employees, at a fixed rate capped at 10%, with a goal of one new full-time job per $20,000 lent. It takes applications in cycles; the fall 2026 portal is closed.
  • San Diego, California: the city's regional revolving loan fund lists loans of $10,000 to $95,000 at 7.5%, up to 6.5 years, with no payments for the first six months. The loan must create or keep jobs in the city. You fill out an interest form and the city contacts you as funding becomes available.
  • Atlanta, Georgia: Invest Atlanta runs several funds for businesses in the city, including a Community Loan Fund ($10,000 to $50,000 at 3% fixed) and commercial down payment help ($25,000 to $250,000 at 1% to 3%) for buying a building.

Who qualifies

Every fund sets its own rules, but the same conditions come up again and again:

  • Location. The business, or the property, must be in the city, county or district.
  • A bank gap. Many funds want to see that a bank said no, or would only fund part of the project.
  • Jobs. Expect to state how many jobs the loan creates or keeps.
  • Your own money in. Down payments of 5% to 20% are common in the examples above.
  • Ability to repay. These are loans, not grants. You'll still need tax returns, financial statements and cash flow that covers the payment. Personal guarantees are common.

How to find the funds near you

  1. Start with your city and county. Search your city or county name plus "small business loan" or "revolving loan fund", or call the economic development office. Many funds are listed only there.
  2. Check EDA's list. The EDA revolving loan fund page links a sortable spreadsheet of RLF recipients, with each one's city, state, website and lending area. EDA also suggests contacting your regional office.
  3. Find your Certified Development Company. SBA keeps a list of CDCs. Ask the one serving your area what local funds it runs or knows of.
  4. Look up CDFIs. The CDFI Fund publishes a list of certified CDFIs you can sort by location, with contact details.
  5. Ask a free adviser. SBA's local assistance finder shows your nearest Small Business Development Center, SCORE chapter or Women's Business Center, which offer free or low-cost counseling. Ask them which local lenders they see owners use.
  6. Ask your bank. Bankers often partner with local funds on gap deals. Ask whether they've closed loans alongside a city, county or CDC fund.

When you reach a fund, ask: is money available now, what's the size range, the rate and fees, what jobs or location rules apply, and how long approval takes. Some take about 90 days.
