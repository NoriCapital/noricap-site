State small business loan programs (SSBCI): what states offer and how to find yours

Every state runs small business loan and investment programs paid for by a federal fund called SSBCI. You rarely apply to the state. You apply to a bank, credit union, CDFI or investor, and the state program helps them say yes. Here's how these programs work, who qualifies, the deadlines, and how to find the one in your state.

Checked November 18, 2026

The short answer

  • What it is: the State Small Business Credit Initiative, a "nearly $10 billion program" run by the U.S. Treasury that funds state, territory and Tribal programs for small businesses.
  • How you get it: usually through a participating lender or investor. The state backs the loan or invests alongside private money.
  • Where to look: Treasury's list of programs and contacts, filterable by state.
  • Timing: Treasury stops processing state requests for more money after December 31, 2027, and some states have already used their early rounds. Ask now, not later.

Where SSBCI came from

The first SSBCI was created by the Small Business Jobs Act of 2010. The $1.5 billion program was administered by the Secretary of the Treasury from 2010 through September 27, 2017. The American Rescue Plan Act of 2021 brought it back with an appropriation of $10 billion for another round of funding. Part of that money is set aside for businesses owned by socially and economically disadvantaged people and for very small businesses, which the program defines as those with fewer than 10 employees, including sole proprietors and independent contractors.

By March 31, 2026, Treasury had approved 56 states and territories and 89 Tribal or joint Tribal applications, with over $8.9 billion in allocations and 335 programs. About 64% of the money went to loan programs and about 36% to equity and venture capital.

The five kinds of state program

Each state picks its own mix. Treasury describes five types. Knowing which one your state runs tells you who to call.

1. Capital access programs

These "provide portfolio insurance in the form of a loan loss reserve fund into which the lender and borrower contribute." The state matches what goes in. Under the program rules, the lender and borrower together put in up to 7% of the loan, and the loan can't be more than $5 million (CRS). Rates and terms are still set between you and the lender. Good for smaller loans a bank finds a bit too risky.

2. Loan participation programs

The state buys part of a loan your lender makes, or lends alongside it (Treasury). The bank has less of its own money at risk, so it can lend more or on better terms. This is the biggest single type, at 34% of program money.

3. Loan guarantee programs

The state promises to repay the lender part of the loan if you default, which reduces "the lender's exposure on a loan." It works much like an SBA guarantee, but run by your state.

4. Collateral support programs

These "provide cash collateral" for businesses whose property or equipment, as the bank values it, falls short of what the loan needs.

5. Equity and venture capital programs

The state invests in young, high-growth companies, often through or alongside private funds. These are for startups raising investment, not for most owner-led businesses that want a loan.

Worked examples (made-up numbers to show how each works)

Capital access: $200,000 loan Borrower puts in 2% = $4,000 Lender puts in 2% = $4,000 State matches the $8,000 = $8,000 Reserve behind the loan = $16,000 (8% of loan) Your cost: the $4,000 fee upfront Loan participation: $1,000,000 loan State buys 25% of the loan = $250,000 Bank's own money at risk = $750,000 Collateral support: $600,000 loan Collateral, as the bank values it = $450,000 Shortfall = $150,000 State pledges cash collateral of up to $150,000

Each state sets its own percentages, fees and caps within Treasury's rules. Ask the program or lender for the actual terms.

Who qualifies

Treasury's rules set the outer limits; each state can be stricter. Under those rules (CRS summary):

  • Loan and guarantee programs can't support businesses with more than 750 employees, and should average 500 or fewer.
  • No single loan or investment above $20 million, and the average should be $5 million or less.
  • Capital access loans are capped at $5 million, for businesses with 500 or fewer employees.
  • Each $1 of state money must bring at least $1 of new private lending. Treasury expects up to $10 of private investment for every $1 overall.

In practice, you still need to look like a reasonable borrower: cash flow that covers the payment, clean books and a clear use for the money. The state program helps with collateral or risk; it doesn't replace the bank's credit check.

How to find your state's program

  1. Open Treasury's list. The capital program list (data current as of October 21, 2026) is one table you can filter by state and program type. Each row shows the program, the agency that runs it and a contact email.
  2. Pick the loan programs. Skip equity rows unless you're raising investment. A few examples from the list: the Innovate Alabama Loan Participation Program, Arizona Loan Guarantee Program, Arkansas Capital Access Program and California's Small Business Loan Guarantee Program, run by its Infrastructure and Economic Development Bank.
  3. Read the program page and email the contact. Ask: is money available now, which lenders take part, what size loans, what fees, and how long approval takes.
  4. Go to a participating lender. Ask your own bank first whether it uses the program. If not, ask the program for its list of lenders, CDFIs or credit unions.

Deadlines and why to ask soon

States get their money from Treasury in three tranches. Treasury expects to stop processing disbursement requests submitted after December 31, 2027, and its authority to run the program ends on March 11, 2028. A state that doesn't use at least 80% of its first tranche within three years can lose the rest. In October 2025 Treasury confirmed it will not be moving the expenditure deadline earlier, as some had feared.

Money already sent to states remains available after March 11, 2028, per Treasury's updated FAQ. But individual programs open, close and change as money runs out. Always confirm with the program before you apply.
