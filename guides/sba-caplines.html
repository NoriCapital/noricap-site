SBA CAPLines: working capital lines for contractors and seasonal businesses

CAPLines are SBA-guaranteed lines of credit for cash needs that come and go: a busy season, a big contract, a house you're building to sell, or receivables that pile up faster than customers pay. They go up to $5 million and run up to 10 years. Here's how each type works, what it costs, and how to ask for one.

Checked November 24, 2026

The short answer

  • What they are: lines of credit inside SBA's 7(a) program, for short-term and cyclical working capital. They can be revolving or non-revolving.
  • Four types: Seasonal, Contract, Builders and Working Capital.
  • Limit: $5 million. SBA guarantees 85% of a line of $150,000 or less and 75% of a larger one.
  • Term: up to 10 years for Seasonal, Contract and Working Capital lines. Builders lines run up to 5 years plus the time it takes to build.
  • Rates: negotiated, but capped at prime plus 3% to 6.5% depending on size, like other 7(a) loans.
  • Who gives them: banks and other SBA lenders, not SBA itself. Not every SBA lender offers CAPLines, so ask.

All rules here come from SBA's lending rulebook, SOP 50 10 8.1, effective October 1, 2026, unless another source is linked.

Why CAPLines exist

A normal term loan gives you a lump sum and a fixed monthly payment. That fits a truck or a building. It fits badly when the need rises and falls: a landscaper buying stock in March, a contractor paying crews for months before the customer pays, a manufacturer whose receivables jump with a big order.

CAPLines are built for that pattern. You draw when the cost hits and pay down when the cash comes in. Because SBA guarantees part of the line, a lender can say yes to a business it would turn down on its own, or offer a bigger line or a longer term.

The four types

1. Seasonal CAPLine

For the build-up of inventory and receivables before and during your busy season, and the extra labor that goes with it. SBA says the money is "solely to finance the seasonal increases of accounts receivable and inventory". It can't be used to keep the business running through the slow months or to refinance debt.

To qualify, the business must have been in operation for at least 12 calendar months and be able to show "a definite pattern of seasonal activity". Principal is repaid as soon as the cash from seasonal sales comes in. Interest is usually paid monthly.

2. Contract CAPLine

For the direct labor, materials and related overhead of one or more specific contracts or purchase orders. It suits contractors, subcontractors and service firms that must spend money long before the customer pays.

The lender normally takes an assignment of the contract and its payments. When the customer pays, the money goes first to interest on the line and the rest to the balance. Payments on one contract can't be used to repay a different one. The line can't pay for the profit or mark-up on the job, permanent working capital, equipment, overdue taxes, or a contract that is already well under way.

3. Builders CAPLine

For small general contractors and builders constructing or substantially renovating a home or commercial building to sell. It pays the direct costs of construction. Land can be included only if it is no more than 33% of the project cost. It can't fund vacant land or rental property.

The term is up to 60 months plus the expected construction time. Repayment comes within 36 months after each project is finished or when it sells, whichever comes first. Interest must be paid at least twice a year from your own money, not from the loan.

4. Working Capital CAPLine

A revolving line backed by your receivables and inventory, for everyday working capital. It fits businesses that sell on credit and carry stock, and that need the line to grow as sales grow.

The lender takes a first lien on receivables and inventory. Larger lines are tracked with a borrowing base certificate, a regular report of what customers owe you and what stock you hold. SBA requires one at least quarterly for lines of $2 million or less and monthly above that. Invoices more than 90 days past due don't count, and inventory can support no more than 50% of its value. Because the lender watches this collateral closely, it may charge extra servicing fees on these lines; SBA says they must be reasonable and no higher than on the lender's similar non-SBA loans.

How big a line you can get

For a Working Capital CAPLine, SBA lets a lender size the line from last year's sales: divide prior-year net sales by 365 to get daily sales, then multiply by the number of days in your sales cycle (the days between paying for work and getting paid). A lender can use its own normal method instead. Seasonal lines are sized from your cash flow projections. Contract and Builders lines are based on the costs of the contract or project.

For lines of $50,000 or less, collateral isn't required. Above that, expect liens on the receivables, inventory, contracts or property the line pays for, and personal guarantees from the owners, as on other 7(a) loans.

Interest rates

CAPLines follow the general 7(a) rate caps. For variable rates, the most a lender can charge is a base rate (usually prime) plus a margin:

Line size Highest variable rate $50,000 or less Prime + 6.5% $50,001 to $250,000 Prime + 6.0% $250,001 to $350,000 Prime + 4.5% Over $350,000 Prime + 3.0%

The bank prime rate was 7.00% in the Federal Reserve's November 23, 2026 release. These are ceilings, not quotes. A strong borrower should be offered less.

Fees

SBA charges an upfront guarantee fee on the guaranteed part of the line. The lender pays it and usually passes it on to you. For loans approved from October 1, 2026 (fiscal year 2027), as summarized by the lenders' trade group NAGGL:

Line size and term Fee on guaranteed part Term of 12 months or less 0.25% Over 12 months, $150,000 or less 2% Over 12 months, $150,001 to $700K 3%

That makes a one-season line of 12 months or less cheap to set up, which suits Seasonal and single-job Contract lines. Larger lines carry higher fees; check NAGGL's summary for loans over $700,000.

Worked examples

These businesses and figures are made up to show the arithmetic. Your lender sets the real line size, rate and fee.

Working Capital CAPLine, 5-year revolving line Prior-year net sales $2,190,000 Daily sales: $2,190,000 ÷ 365 = $6,000 Sales cycle to finance 45 days Line size: $6,000 × 45 = $270,000 SBA guarantee: $270,000 × 75% = $202,500 Upfront fee: $202,500 × 3% = $6,075 Highest rate: 7.00% + 4.5% = 11.50% B. Seasonal CAPLine, 12 months or less Line size $120,000 SBA guarantee: $120,000 × 85% = $102,000 Upfront fee: $102,000 × 0.25% = $255 Highest rate: 7.00% + 6.0% = 13.00% Interest if $90,000 is drawn on average for 5 months: $90,000 × 13.00% × 5 ÷ 12 = $4,875

In example B the business pays interest only on what it draws, for the months it uses it, and the line is cleared when the season's sales come in. Model your own payments with the loan payment calculator, and see how much debt your earnings can support with the how much can I borrow calculator.

CAPLines or something else?

  • SBA Express line: faster, since the lender decides on its own, but capped at $500,000 with a 50% guarantee. See our SBA Express guide.
  • Regular bank line: simpler and cheaper if your bank will give you one on its own terms. CAPLines help when it won't, or won't give enough.
  • Standard 7(a) term loan: better for equipment, real estate or buying a business. CAPLines can't pay for fixed assets.
  • Factoring or cash advances: quick, but usually far more expensive. A CAPLine is worth asking about first if you have time.

How to apply

  1. Find a lender that makes CAPLines. Start with your own bank. SBA's free Lender Match connects you with other SBA lenders. Ask which CAPLine types they offer.
  2. Show the cycle. Bring a cash flow forecast that shows when money goes out and when it comes back. A seasonal business should show the pattern from past years; a contractor should bring the contract, the job budget and the payment schedule. The free 13-week cash flow template is a good start.
  3. Get your receivables in order. For a Working Capital line, the lender will want an aging report. Old, unpaid invoices don't count toward what you can borrow.
  4. Bring the usual package: tax returns, recent financial statements, a debt schedule and what the money is for. Our loan application checklist lists it all, and the DSCR guide explains how lenders test repayment.
  5. Compare offers. Ask for the rate, the guarantee fee, any servicing or monitoring fees, the term and the collateral, and check each against the caps above.

This guide is general information, not financial or legal advice. SBA rules, limits, rates and fees change, so confirm the details with your lender or SBA before you apply.
