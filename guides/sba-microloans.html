SBA Microloans: who offers them and how to apply

An SBA Microloan is a loan of up to $50,000 from a nonprofit community lender, not a bank. It suits a newer or smaller business that needs equipment, inventory or working capital and has been turned down, or never tried, at a bank. You apply to the local lender, and it makes the decision.

Checked November 23, 2026

The short answer

  • Amount: up to $50,000. SBA says the average microloan is about $13,000.
  • Who lends: SBA-approved nonprofit community-based organizations, called intermediaries. SBA lends them the money; they lend it to you.
  • Rates: set by each lender, generally between 8% and 13%, with a legal ceiling (below).
  • Term: the regulation says each microloan "must be repaid within seven years". SBA's program page still says six, so ask the lender what term it offers.
  • Uses: working capital, inventory, supplies, furniture, fixtures, machinery and equipment. Not to pay existing debts or buy real estate.
  • Where to start: SBA's list of microlenders, filtered by your state.

How the program works

SBA doesn't lend to you directly. It lends money to nonprofit intermediaries, which then make small loans to businesses in their area. Per SBA, these lenders have "experience in lending as well as management and technical assistance", and the rules require each one to have at least a year of experience giving technical help to its borrowers. SBA also gives them grant money to pay for that help.

That is the real difference from a bank loan: the lender is set up to help with things like a business plan, bookkeeping or cash flow, not just to lend. Whether that help is included or required is up to each lender, so ask.

The lender is in charge of the credit decision. In SBA's words, "SBA-approved lenders make all credit decisions and set all terms for your microloan." The regulation adds that "SBA does not review Microloans for creditworthiness."

Who qualifies

The program helps "small businesses and certain not-for-profit childcare centers" start up and expand. Your business must be small under SBA's size standards and be a type SBA lends to. Beyond that, each lender sets its own standards. The program is built for startups and small businesses, so a microlender may consider an owner a bank would pass over, but each one sets its own bar and none is required to say yes.

What lenders usually want, per SBA: "some type of collateral as well as the personal guarantee of the business owner." Collateral might be the equipment you're buying or other business assets.

Loan size: why many loans are smaller than $50,000

The $50,000 is a ceiling, and the rules nudge lenders lower. The regulation says "Generally, Intermediaries should not make a Microloan of more than $10,000 to any borrower." A lender can go above $10,000 with more review, and above $20,000 only if the borrower can't get credit elsewhere at comparable rates and shows good prospects for success. And no borrower may owe one intermediary more than $50,000 at a time.

Interest rates and fees

Each lender sets its own rate, typically 8% to 13%. The regulation caps it. The maximum is the rate the intermediary pays SBA on its own loan, plus:

Your loan size Highest rate allowed More than $10,000 Lender's SBA rate + 7.75 points $10,000 or less Lender's SBA rate + 8.5 points

Source: 13 CFR 120.707(c). The lender's SBA rate is tied to the five-year Treasury rate, set below it (13 CFR 120.706(c)), so the ceiling moves with market rates. You won't see the lender's SBA rate yourself; the useful step is to ask for the rate in writing and compare it with the 8% to 13% range.

Fees: SBA charges intermediaries no fees on the money it lends them. The rules we read don't set a cap on fees a lender charges you, so ask for a full list (application, closing, filing) and include them when you compare.

Two takeaways. Across the typical range, the rate changes the total interest by about $4,500 to $5,500 on this loan, so it's worth comparing lenders. And a longer term lowers the monthly payment by about $47 to $49 but adds about $1,200 to $2,100 of interest. Pick the payment your cash flow can carry comfortably. How to find a microlender

  1. Use SBA's list. The list of microlenders has a "filter by state or territory served" dropdown. Pick your state to see the lenders that serve it.
  2. Call two or three. Ask: what's your typical loan size and rate, what term, what fees, what collateral, how long a decision takes, and whether you require training or coaching.
  3. Get free help with the paperwork. Ask the lender what help it offers, or ask a local Small Business Development Center to help with the business plan and projections a lender will ask for.
  4. If you need more than $50,000, look at an SBA Express or 7(a) loan instead; our SBA programs guide compares them, and local loan funds may also fit.

How to apply

  1. Write down what the money is for, item by item, with prices. Lenders want to see that it buys equipment, inventory or working capital, not old debts.
  2. Get your numbers in order. Expect to share tax returns, recent profit and loss statements and a balance sheet if you have them, bank statements, a personal financial statement, and for a startup, a business plan with projections. Our loan application checklist covers the full list.
  3. Show how you'll repay. Add the new payment to your monthly costs and check your cash still covers it in slow months. The free 13-week cash flow template helps you see that week by week.
  4. Compare offers and read the terms: rate, total fees, term, collateral, and any prepayment rules.

This guide is general information, not financial or legal advice. SBA rules, lenders and terms change, and each lender sets its own credit standards, so confirm the details with the lender before you apply.
